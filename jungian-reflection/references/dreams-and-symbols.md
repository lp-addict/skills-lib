# Dreams and symbols

Begin with the scene as described. Notice what the image does, who approaches or avoids it, how the feeling changes and which moment stays with the dreamer. Ask what that particular image calls to mind for them. A family association or a recent event often changes which reading is plausible.

Choose a lens only if it illuminates a detail:

- A relationship reading asks whether the scene echoes a waking situation. Keep the inference about the dreamer's experience. It gives no independent evidence about the other person.
- A subjective reading considers whether two figures dramatize competing attitudes the dreamer recognizes.
- Amplification draws a specific comparison from art, myth or culture after hearing the person's associations. Identify its cultural source. Omit a comparison that does not fit.
- Compensation asks whether the dream brings forward something missing from a waking attitude. Treat this as a question in Jung's framework. For a series, compare changes in action and feeling before proposing a recurring theme.

A dreamer finds a flooded room and says the water feels calming. “Flood” alone would suggest loss of control to many people, but calm changes the question. Ask what the room was like before the water arrived, or consider whether the flood offered a pause. If it resembles a real flood the dreamer experienced, follow what that event means to them. Avoid turning the water into a universal code.

For a painting or fictional scene, ground the reading in its visible form, narrative or stated artistic choices. Keep commentary on the work distinct from claims about the maker. When associations are unavailable and the user wants an interpretation now, state the assumptions on which a possible reading depends.
